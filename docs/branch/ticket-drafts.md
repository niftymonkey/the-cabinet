## 1. Comment on #39, the first tuning round

```markdown

### The grave barely grows on a fresh run, and one hit takes back more than mowing can return

On a fresh run the grave sits near its starting size for most of the run. One contact hit shrinks it by three units, and winning that back means swallowing about thirty perfectly fresh corpses, or about fifty-four at the freshness corpses are actually swallowed at. A boss feast now gives back about one and a half hits where it used to give back ten. A whole run's supply of corpses, every one of them swallowed and no hits taken at all, tops out near a size of fifty-five against a ceiling of sixty-seven and a half. So a hit feels permanent, mowing does not read as recovery, and the grave stops being the thing that shows a player how the run is going.

Need: growth paid in and size taken out stand in a relation a player can feel, so that mowing visibly grows the grave, a hit is something a player can mow back, and a feast is worth diving for as a comeback and not only for the reservoir it fills.

Done when: a player who mows well sees the grave grow through a run rather than sit at its starting size, a player who takes a hit can win the size back by mowing rather than only by reaching a feast, a fresh run can grow the grave well clear of where it started, and the figures behind each of those come from batches on which the sharp and the sloppy hand agree.

The arithmetic. The climb from the starting size (27) to the ceiling (67.5) is 40.5 units. A fresh corpse pays 40.5 over 400, which is 0.10125. The hit's shrink is 3. A feast pays 45 corpses, which is 4.556 units. On the day the feast was ruled at eight to ten corpses the economy was 80 corpses to the ceiling, so a corpse paid 0.506 and one hit cost six corpses. The feast against a hit is 1.5 on both days, so slice 9 restored that ruling faithfully. What moved underneath it is the corpse against a hit, from six to thirty, when the corpses-to-ceiling row went from 80 to 400, and the 300-corpse feast hid it for a week.

What the batch says, read with the right player. The batch is played by the harness hand, which walks at the nearest food on every tick it has room to. It is not a person: it takes the nearest body rather than the freshest, it never routes, and it gives up the move whenever clearance is short. On the fresh rig it swallows about 72 corpses a run (about 4 units at the freshness paid), takes 2 feasts (about 9 units) and takes 9 contact hits (27 units). Its median peak size on that rig fell from 67.5 to 29.7 across slice 9. Supply is the harder wall: at slice 2 the same corner's median run saw about 190 corpses reach any end, and a perfect collector who is never hit finishes near 55. On the maxed rig the margin over the climb fell from four climbs to one and a quarter, and a few runs now finish under the ceiling.

The caveat that keeps it honest. The kill rate is the hand's too, and a person who dives takes power-ups sooner, thickens the storm sooner and makes more corpses. So the figures size the shift rather than deliver a verdict, and the first read that matters is the one from your own play: whether the grave ever gets big, and whether one hit feels like something you can mow back.

The measure this asks for. Growth paid in by source (corpses, feasts) against size taken out by hits, per section, read from batches across both hands. The rows to sweep together are the hit's shrink, the corpses-to-ceiling economy and the feast's payout, because slice 9 showed that no one of them is the fault alone. All three are tuning-record rows already, carried in every tape header, so nothing needs a rebuild to ask the question.

Prior art, looked up. Ernest Adams, "The Designer's Notebook: Preventing the Downward Spiral" (https://www.gamedeveloper.com/design/the-designer-s-notebook-preventing-the-downward-spiral), suggests not letting damage impair performance and including a repair mechanism. This game impairs on purpose: a hit narrows the mouth and a narrower mouth tips fewer corpses, and slice 9's own batch shows it (739 corpses down to 611 on the maxed sharp corner). It also has a force the other way, since a smaller grave is harder to hit. The 300-corpse feast was the repair mechanism and it is now one and a half hits. Brotato's boss crate heals a player fully (https://brotato.wiki.spellsandguns.com/Loot_Crate). Katamari Damacy considered a harsher crumbling katamari and did not ship it (https://www.gamedeveloper.com/design/postmortem-the-singular-design-of-namco-s-katamari-damacy-2004-). Neither is a number for this game; both show size-or-health games keeping the loss from a mistake small against the rate of regrowth.

One thing not to do. Nothing changes before it is measured, and the design record says the same: the rows are data and cheap to move, and tuning waits for evidence.

Evidence: the game design gate on the built branch, `local/148-gates/game-design.md` finding 2, with `apps/hungry-grave/src/game/tuning.ts` and `docs/branch/records/after-slice-9-batch.md`.
```

```markdown
### A corpse fades into the new earth, and the stalest food is the loudest

The ground's colours and the grave's are your rulings from this branch and nothing here touches them. The mobs and the corpses were valued for a black field, where things glow on the dark. Food is the sprite caught in the middle, because food is the only one whose brightness is animated. Separately, the north star's readability question guards mob fire only, so a change can dim the thing the central bet depends on and still pass all ten questions; whether to close that gap is yours.

### The ground can come back blank after a phone switches away from the game

On a phone, switching to another app, locking the screen or running the phone low on memory makes the browser throw away the surface the game draws on. The ground is painted once at the start of a run into a picture that lives only on that surface, so it comes back empty, and nothing ever asks for it to be painted again. A player returns to a run in progress and plays the rest of it over nothing. Two more costs sit on the same painting: it carries a large extra buffer for as long as the run lasts, and it repaints on every frame of a window resize or a phone rotation rather than once at the end of it.

Need: a run survives the phone taking the screen away, and what the ground costs in memory and time is known on a phone rather than on a desktop.

Done when: a player who switches to another app mid-run and comes back sees the same ground they left, turning a phone repaints the ground once rather than on every frame of the turn, and the ground's memory and paint time are figures measured on a real phone.

This is a run-breaking fault on the screen this game is for, which is why it sits so high. It arrived with slice 8, where the prototype's ground went on the whole field.

The cause, read in the Pixi source and confirmed by lookup, not reproduced in a browser. The baked ground is a `RenderTexture` with no CPU-side resource. On `webglcontextrestored` Pixi's `GlTextureSystem.contextChange` drops every GL texture and rebuilds each one lazily from its source's resource; a render texture has none, so it comes back empty. `bakedAt` in `BackgroundRenderer.ts` still equals the wanted resolution, so `askForABake` never asks again. `git grep -i "contextlost\|contextrestored\|contextChange" -- src` finds no handling anywhere in the app. iOS Safari loses the context when a tab is backgrounded, the device locks, or memory is tight (maplibre-gl-js issue 3241, WebKit bug 286297, MDN `webglcontextlost`). The grave's own bakes are 2D canvases behind a `CanvasSource`, which Pixi uploads again after a restore, which is why the grave survives and the ground does not.

Two cures, either of which closes it: null `bakedAt` on the renderer's `contextChange` runner so the next pass asks again, or copy the bake once to a canvas-backed texture (`renderer.extract.canvas`), which survives a restore the way the grave's does.

Why the other two costs belong with it. `generateTexture` is called with `antialias: true`, and `GlRenderTargetAdaptor` then allocates a multisampled renderbuffer at a fixed four samples that lives as long as the render target. At the desktop probe's 1170 by 1647 that is about 7.7 MB of colour plus about 31 MB of samples; at the code's own ceiling (resolution 3, 1620 by 2280) about 15 MB plus about 59 MB. Whether Pixi's resource collection frees the multisample buffer while the texture is in use was not confirmed, so it is a thing to measure on a device rather than a proven leak. The canvas copy above releases it either way, which is why one cure closes two of the three. Separately, `groundResolution` returns a continuous number and `askForABake` compares it with `===`, so every frame of a window drag and every orientation change repaints about four thousand shapes and bakes again (28.5 ms median on a desktop software renderer, unmeasured on a phone); quantising the resolution to a step and baking only when the step changes makes the JSDoc's "asks once a run" true.

Also unmeasured and not a fault: the bake hitch at run start lands on the second frame of a run, inside the lead-in, and the fixed-step accumulator buys the lost ticks back.

Evidence: the tech architecture gate finding 2, `local/148-gates/tech-architecture.md`, against Pixi 8.19 source in `node_modules/pixi.js/lib`.

Need: a ruling, reached from a held frame of it in play, on whether claimed ground may cover the grave. This is read from the draw order and nobody has seen it happen, and moving a layer is a large change, so it is a question for this ticket's grill and not a rule yet.

Done when: Mark has seen a held frame of a patch laid over the grave and has ruled on it, and if he rules that the grave stays clear, a patch laid over the grave leaves the hole and anything falling into it visible.

### A tuning number decides how often the grave's art is repainted

The grave's art is repainted whenever its size has moved far enough, and since the grave now grows smoothly its size moves on every tick while growth is owed. During a feast's one-second swell, the moment the game most wants to look smooth, the art is repainted about eleven times, two surfaces each time. How often that happens is set by a number the tuning round is meant to move for reasons that have nothing to do with drawing, so moving it moves the cost of drawing and nothing says so. The repaint has only ever been timed on a desktop.

Need: how often the art is repainted is decided by what it costs to draw, and the tuning number that sets the swell does not quietly set it.

Done when: the repaint cost is a figure measured on a phone at the moment of a feast, the grave swells through a feast with no visible hitch on a phone, and whether the repaint has to be parted from the swell's tuning number is decided from that figure.

Measure first and change nothing before that: the number involved is a ported value from the prototype, and the measurement this asks for is a phone measurement.

The arithmetic. `HOLE_REBUILD_STEP` is 0.4 in `graveDrawingValues.ts` and its JSDoc says "a swallow pays about a tenth of a unit, so the hole is baked about every fourth one", which was true before slice 9. Now the size moves by the swell, 0.075 a tick while anything is owed, so the hole is baked every 5.3 ticks, about eleven times across a feast's 1.01 second swell. Slice 6 measured a bake at 2 to 8 ms on a desktop at device scale 3, with the texture upload outside the timer. A phone is unmeasured.

Why this is a coupling and not just a cost. `growth.swellPerSecond` is a tuning-record row the first tuning round is meant to move, and it currently sets render cadence as a side effect. The stale JSDoc is on the fix agent's list already; the coupling is this finding.

The way out costs no look. Slice 6's follow-up already stretches the art between bakes, and at a 0.4 step the stretch is under two percent, so the step can widen, or become a share of the size, with no visible change.

Evidence: the tech architecture gate finding 3, `local/148-gates/tech-architecture.md`.
```

```markdown
### A falling body can draw over the lip of the grave on its way down

When a body falls into the grave, the drawing does not cut it to the shape of the opening. A body longer than the opening can draw across the near lip on its way down, where a real hole would hide it behind the edge. The rule is unaffected: the fall's own arithmetic still folds the body to fit, so this is what a held frame of a fall looks like rather than a question about what gets swallowed.

Need: a body going down into the grave is hidden by the near edge the way the ground hides it.

Done when: at every grave size, a body falling into the grave is drawn only inside the opening, and a held frame of the longest body falling into the smallest grave shows nothing crossing the lip.

### A second stage's last boss would end a run on a blank frozen field

Whether a winning run gets its ending scene is decided in two places that do not ask the same question. The view that draws the scene begins it only when the boss that died is the Undertaker, by name. The code that holds the field for the scene holds it whenever the run was won, whoever died. Today the Undertaker is the only boss that ends a run, so the two agree and nothing is wrong. The day a stage ends on anybody else, a player who wins watches a frozen, fading field for nearly three seconds with nothing drawn on it, and then the end screen.

Need: one place decides that a winning death has a scene, and the view that draws the scene does not know a boss by name.

Done when: a run won against any boss that ends the run either shows the ending scene or goes straight to the end screen, never holds an empty field, and a test fails if a view decides whether a scene happens by a boss's name.

This is the other half of one finding whose first half went to #141, the two screens' hand-mirroring: the same refactor is what exposes this second owner. Every reachable state is consistent today, so this is work to schedule and not a fix to make now.

The two owners. `EndingSceneRenderer.begin` opens with `if (killed.boss !== 'undertaker') return false`, which is a view naming a cast member. `runEnding.holdsForTheScene` is `execution.stop === null && run.ending === 'victory'`, which is the right question. Each has its own test, so both are pinned as they stand.

The shape. The rules already say which death ends the run: the `victory` event rides the same frame as that `bossKilled`. So the scene should begin from that and the view should stop knowing names.

It matters beyond a second stage. The product vision gate raised the same thing from the pool-or-club angle: the design record says the ending scene is "the Undertaker's alone", which is fine with one stage and one ending, and the scene should key on the death that ends the run so a later stage's last boss inherits it.

### Nothing would catch a restyle of the ported grave and ground drawings

About twelve hundred lines of drawing code were ported line for line from the prototype, on a ruling that the grave and the ground must look exactly like it. What the tests check is shape: the order the pieces are painted in, that the side walls come out equal, that the same size paints the same way twice, and a handful of ground colours and counts. A restyled painter passes every one of those. The only thing that says the drawing is still the prototype's is a pair of side-by-side screenshots, read by eye once. The helpers inside the ported files are reachable by anything in the app as well, so they can spread and the port stops being a contained thing.

Need: a change to how the ported drawings look fails a test, and the ported code stays where it was put.

Done when: a change to what the ported painters draw fails a test that names the drawing, the ported drawing files are imported only by each other and by the two renderers that use them, and a test fails if anything else imports them.

This belongs in front of any restyle of the ports rather than after one, because a restyle is the change it exists to protect against. That the grave and the ground look exactly like the prototype's is your ruling, and this is what holds it.

What exists today. Five grave tests (paint order, equal side walls, "painted the same way every time", the mouth grows with size) and eight ground tests (colours, counts, the seam, the resolution). "The same way every time" is repeatability inside one process, which a restyled painter satisfies just as well.

Why the fence is cheap. The tests already hand the painters a recording canvas, so a pinned digest of the recorded operation log at two or three sizes is a small addition. The import fence covers `graveWalls.ts`, `graveLip.ts`, `graveMouth.ts`, `graveCanvas.ts` and `groundPainting.ts`, which `git grep` shows are imported only by each other and by the two renderers today, so the fence pins a fact rather than forcing a move. It also keeps `clamp`, `lerp` and `makeRandom` in `graveCanvas.ts` from becoming the app's utility drawer.

Evidence: the tech architecture gate finding 6, `local/148-gates/tech-architecture.md`.

### Two renderers read the browser's layout on every frame to work out their own scale

Two of the field's renderers each work out how many screen pixels one unit of the field is worth by asking the browser for the canvas's size on every frame, and each keeps its own record of what it last painted at. The code doing it is the same in both, word for word. The screen that owns where the field sits already knows the one fact the two are missing, and that fact changes only when the window changes. So two views each hold a data source, a loop subscription and change detection, which the house rule says a view never holds, and one of them carries an extra ask-then-paint step that exists only because the frame callback is the one place that renderer can be reached.

Need: a renderer is handed the scale it draws at, and the screen that owns the layout is the one place that works it out.

Done when: no renderer asks the browser for a layout measurement, no renderer keeps its own record of when to repaint, and the field draws at the same scale it does today at every window size.
```
