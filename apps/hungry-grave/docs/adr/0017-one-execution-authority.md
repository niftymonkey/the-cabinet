# One execution authority

Every executed tick crosses one function, and reaching around it is a build failure rather than a convention. `executeTick(execution, command)` runs the tick and hands back the events it produced, and `Execution` is a plain record rather than a class, holding the run, an ordered listener array, the stage watch and a broken-invariant handler. `advance`, the bot's `runPolicy` and the golden scenario all become loops over it. Listener order is array order, so the fan-out stays deterministic and one test cannot leak into another.

Three paths reach the simulation today, so a recording seam anywhere else has a hole in it by construction: `advance()`'s inner loop in `src/game/advance.ts`, `stepChecked()` in the invariant harness, and four raw `step()` calls in `src/app/runHandoff.test.ts`. Mark reshaped the question rather than answering it when the closest available seam, `CommandSource`, was offered (2026-08-22): if legitimate execution paths can bypass the recording seam, he would rather refactor the architecture to create the correct seam than accept an observability gap, and observability is treated here the way testability is, where the current implementation is not a constraint. The boundary means "this is the command the simulation is executing" rather than "this is where we happened to obtain a command", and he named those as subtly different responsibilities. The refactor is a merge of wrappers that already exist rather than an invention.

The guarantee is held by a lint rule and not by this document. `step` stays exported and any module in `src/game` can still call it, and `src/boundary.test.ts` cannot express the rule either, because `mayReach: ["game"]` is prefix-matched and covers `game/step` by construction. So the fence is an ESLint `no-restricted-imports` rule naming `step` across `src/**` with the execution module exempted, the same file-scoped override pattern `eslint.config.mjs` already uses to carve out `Math.random` for `math.ts` and `rng.ts`. **Two details of the rule's shape are load-bearing and the first draft of this fence got both wrong.** It is written as a `patterns` entry rather than as a `paths` entry naming `./step`, because `paths` matches the literal string as written, so a rule on `./step` blocks nothing spelled `../step` from `src/game/lines/`. **`patterns` matches gitignore-style globs against the import source string too and never resolves a module**, so the fence is containment rather than proof: it catches every spelling anybody writes today, and a re-export barrel or a future tsconfig path alias would walk through it with nothing failing. The rule is written knowing that, and the four-spelling test is what keeps it honest. And it covers `src/**` rather than `src/game/**`, because `src/game/**` does not reach the place the rule was actually broken: the four raw calls are in `src/app/runHandoff.test.ts`, and `src/boundary.test.ts` separately permits `src/dev` to reach `game/step`, so both of the roots that can break it sit outside a `src/game/**` scope. `eslint.config.mjs` already documents this same hole for the math fence, where the `SteerSource` closure in `src/app` is out of reach. The four importers today are `src/game/advance.ts`, `src/game/advance.test.ts`, `src/game/invariants.ts` and `src/app/runHandoff.test.ts`, and all four are resolved by this dispatch. This matters because the previous version of the rule was exactly the kind that fails silently: `stepChecked`'s own comment says every sim test steps through it and never through `step` directly, that rule was Hungry Grave ADR 0013 plus a comment enforced by nothing, and `src/app/runHandoff.test.ts` had already broken it four times without anybody noticing.

Passing a listener array as an extra parameter to `executeTick`, `advance` and `runPolicy` was designed and rejected. It works, and it makes the listeners optional at three call sites, and optional is how they get forgotten. Putting the authority in a new folder that may import RxJS was rejected on the boundary rules: `src/game` and `src/dev` both carry `mayImport: []`, so the bot, the digest and every sim test could no longer reach the authority without widening that rule and pulling RxJS into every headless test run, and an invariant that must throw synchronously to fail a test cannot live behind a subscriber whose throw takes the other subscribers down with it. Hungry Grave ADR 0020 carries what RxJS is for instead.

The invariants run on every tick, in every build, with no flag and no listener opt-out (ruled by Mark 2026-08-23). Either escape hatch makes the honesty of a tape depend on which build recorded it, and a tape whose honesty is conditional is worth less than no tape. The `?invariants=` switch on `advance` is measurement-only scaffolding and does not survive into the shipped game: the optimised check implementation and the observation capability are production architecture and outlive the switch that measured them.

**The switch does have to outlive the recorder long enough to prove this ruling, though.** Mark ruled on 2026-08-23 that this dispatch must be able to perform and retrieve a trustworthy apples-to-apples confirming measurement rather than leaning on future infrastructure to say whether always-on checking was safe. Apples-to-apples means both readings come off the same instrument, so the checks-off condition has to be reachable on the build that carries the recorder: a baseline read by eye off an older deploy and an after-reading read off a structured row are two instruments and cannot be differenced. So the switch is removed **after** the confirming play rather than before it, in its own commit (ruled by Mark 2026-08-23). The order is fixed: the recorder exists, then the checks-off reading is taken, then the checks-on reading, then the confirming play succeeds, and only then is the switch removed. **It is a temporary experimental control and never a feature being preserved**, and it must never be reachable in a player build.

**Never reachable in a player build is a technical requirement rather than a naming convention.** A URL parameter is reachable by anybody who types one, so a player build must not honour these parameters even when they are supplied by hand. That needs a build-time gate rather than a comment, which means the game has **two deployed build flavours**: an instrumentation build that honours the measurement controls, and the player build that does not. Note this does not weaken the measurement, because the two differ only in whether those controls are honoured: the simulation, the invariant checks and the render path are identical code in both, so a reading taken on the instrumentation build is a reading of the code a player runs. The invariants themselves stay ungated in every build, per the paragraph above; the gate is on the controls, never on the checking.

**A fired invariant does not end the run, and the eleven checks do not share one severity.** `Execution` carries an `onBroken` handler. A fired invariant today freezes the canvas unrepainted with no ending, no message and no tape, because pixi 8.19.0's `Ticker.update` has no try/catch around the listener chain and `Application.render` is registered at LOW priority while `GameScreen.update` is NORMAL. The always-on ruling above is untouched and only the response differs. **This supersedes and refines Mark's earlier ruling of 2026-08-23 that a broken invariant stops the run.** That ruling was right about the half it was answering: the frozen canvas is the bug, and a player must get a message and a tape rather than a dead screen. Two things it could not have known were established by reading the checks against the code. The first is that `checkInvariants` runs eleven distinct checks and only some describe a field that cannot be played. The second is that routing the stop through `RunEnding` tells the player the game killed them, since `RunEnding` is `"sealed" | "victory"`, `EndScreen.ts:19` maps sealed to "SEALED SHUT", and sealed shut is this game's word for death. `VISION.md` section 3 requires failure to be legible and earned and section 2 requires the recovery path never to close, and a run ended instantly by a check is neither. Precedent is Unreal's `check()`, which halts, against `ensure()`, which reports and continues.

**A faulted run must be visibly different from a run the player quit, and the difference is kept minimal** (ruled by Mark 2026-08-23). Keeping a fault out of `RunEnding` puts it in the pause-quit category, which is right, and leaves it byte-identical to a quit, which is not: both render `ABANDONED_TITLE`, "THE RUN IS OVER", the same seed and tick lines and the same button, so a fault nine thousand ticks into a good run reads to the player as their own thumb. **No new screen is built.** A fatal fault adds enough to the existing end state to say the run stopped because the instrument found a problem. A recoverable fault gets a minimal indication that a fault occurred and that the run's tuning evidence may therefore be compromised. The reason is not politeness to the player, and it is why the minimal version is enough: Mark is the instrument, and he has to be able to tell **"the game needs tuning" from "the game malfunctioned"** without being told which. A tuning decision taken unknowingly on a broken run is worse than no decision at all. Precedent is Minecraft's `FatalErrorScreen`, a state distinct from both the death screen and quit-to-title because the player's next action differs in each; the counter-example is RimWorld, which logged recoverable exceptions invisibly until its community built HugsLib to surface them.

**A check is fatal when continued execution would be unusable or untrustworthy, and recoverable when execution can safely continue** (ruled by Mark 2026-08-23). The test is semantic safety and never how cosmetic the symptom looks, and each check was classified against the code rather than against its own name. Fatal, six. **No NaN**, because NaN spreads and every comparison against it is false, so containment, culling and collision quietly stop working. **Levels in range**, because a level is an array index rather than a meter: `WISPS_BY_LEVEL` holds six entries against a `MAX_LEVEL` of five, an out-of-range level reads `undefined`, and `lines/bell.ts:107` multiplies that into `push` whose `push <= 0` guard is false for NaN, so it writes NaN straight into `mob.x`. **Entity ids**, because `lines/wisps.ts:89` resolves a target by id and takes the first match, so two live slots sharing an id send a wisp after a mob it never locked onto, and line 98 counts commitments by id so target saturation miscounts as well. **Grave in bounds**, because the player is off the field and collision and the Wall's width arithmetic stop meaning anything. **Size within floor and ceiling**, because size is health and out of range means the seal condition was missed, so a run that should be over keeps playing. **Entity caps**, because pools are pre-allocated at capacity and `takeSlot` never pushes (`caps.ts:54-75`), so the array's shape can only change through code outside the pool API, and once a structural assumption that foundational is violated no other check's answer is trustworthy either. Recoverable, five. **Entities in bounds**, since a stray entity is culled or draws off-screen and nothing reads it wrong. **Freshness in range**, since a corpse pays the wrong amount into a size the fatal check still guards. **Reservoir in range**, since one line's charge is wrong and `payReservoir` clamps it back. **One live ring**, since a bell ring over-expands within one line. **Stage phase**, since spawns repeat or skip while the simulation stays coherent.

**A check records a fault and returns; it does not throw** (ruled by Mark 2026-08-23, superseding the open question this paragraph used to carry). Every invariant check for the tick runs, and the authority sees the complete set of faults for that tick before it decides anything. **Check ordering must not affect which faults are observed**, which is the whole point: the only signal before this ruling was `fail()` throwing a plain `Error`, and `checkInvariants` ran its ten checks in sequence with `entities in bounds`, a recoverable check, sitting fourth, ahead of the fatal `entity caps`, `entity ids` and `levels in range`. Under a throw the first fault aborted every later check in the same tick, so a recoverable fault switched off three fatal ones, and this ADR's own words make a persistent recoverable fault the normal case rather than an edge. **A recoverable fault must never prevent a later fatal safety check from running**, and that property is the reason for the shape rather than a benefit of it.

A fault carries at least **its identity and its severity**. Any fatal fault in the set stops the run under the fatal-fault ruling below. A set holding only recoverable faults is recorded and the run continues.

**An unexpected failure inside the checking machinery itself is a different thing and still throws.** Successfully detecting a violated invariant is the checker working; a checker that cannot run is a bug in the checker, and it must not be swallowed into the same fault list it exists to produce.

**This supersedes the promise that `src/game/invariants.test.ts` survives the harness move untouched**, which Mark accepted in making the ruling. That file asserts `toThrow` on checks now classified recoverable, and those assertions change with the mechanism.

**A dev handler crashes on a fatal fault and reports a recoverable one without stopping the run** (ruled by Mark 2026-08-23). This ADR says a caller's handler may crash loudly in dev and stay quiet in a player's build, and under a throwing checker that meant one behaviour. Under record-and-continue it cannot mean "throw on any fault", because a persistent recoverable fault fires every tick and a dev handler that threw on it would stop a run this ruling says may continue, freezing the canvas exactly as the bug this ADR opens with. **Severity means the same thing everywhere in the system**, which is the principle rather than a convenience: a recoverable fault should be loud and visible in development and must never terminate execution, in any build. Loudness and severity are separate axes, and only the build decides loudness. The notification carries the tick's whole fault set rather than a single failure.

**A fault's identity is append-only from the first tape and is written down as a closed list, never taken from whatever string a `fail()` call happens to carry.** Hungry Grave ADR 0018 puts fault records in the tape's third section, so the identity hardens exactly as `run.ending`'s encoding does, and Hungry Grave ADR 0019 gives that a code map "never by ordinal" for the same reason. The code throws **twelve** identities against eleven checks, because `checkStage` throws `"phase index only increases"` and `"phase tick resets at a boundary"` separately. One pair needs care rather than a rename in passing: the grave check's identity is `"in bounds"` and sits beside a separate `"entities in bounds"`, one fatal and one recoverable, which is the pair a severity table most easily confuses.

**The authority sets the stop reason itself and only notifies `onBroken`.** A caller-supplied handler deciding how a run stops is exactly what the always-on ruling exists to prevent, because which build constructed the `Execution` would then decide what the tape records, and this ADR rejects an optional listener array with "optional is how they get forgotten" and must not then do that. `onBroken` is a notification, so dev can still crash loudly and a player's build can stay quiet, and neither can change the outcome. **Nothing about a fault touches witness-folded `RunState`.** A fatal fault does not set `run.ending`; the stop reason and the fault record are fields on `Execution`, which Hungry Grave ADR 0019 does not fold. That is load-bearing rather than tidy, because `run.ending` is folded, so a fault that wrote it would make a replay under a later severity policy fold a different value and diverge on something that is not a rule of the game. Keeping faults off `RunState` is what lets the witness stay exactly as Hungry Grave ADR 0019 defines it, and a replay reproduces a faulted run tick for tick.

**A fatal fault stops execution for the rest of the frame, and the catch-up clamp is the reason.** The clamp buys up to fifteen ticks in one frame, so one broken invariant would otherwise re-fire fourteen more times inside the frame that caught it. The loop reads the stop condition off `Execution` before each tick. **The loop stops on a fault and deliberately does not stop on `run.ending`** (ruled by Mark 2026-08-23, splitting what an earlier draft of this paragraph called one loop condition). The two look like one fix and are not. Stopping on a fatal fault only ever changes a run the instrument has already declared broken, so it cannot contaminate evidence. Stopping on `run.ending` changes the final score and the tick count a player sees on an ordinary run, and this dispatch's boundary is that nothing a player sees changes. There is a live gap there: neither `advance`'s loop at `advance.ts:65-68` nor `step` reads `run.ending` anywhere today, so a run that seals on tick one of a fifteen-tick frame executes fourteen more ticks after it is over, up to 250 milliseconds of field advancing past the moment of death, and the single frame the player sees is drawn at the end of it. **It is a real bug and it gets its own ticket rather than riding in here**, because fixing it inside the instrument dispatch would move the very before-and-after numbers the instrument is being built to compare.

**Nothing in the tree can catch that gap, which is why it needed a person to find it.** `GameScreen.update` calls `endedIn` only after the whole catch-up budget has run, the golden scenario never ends so it never reaches the code, and `runPolicy` already carries `state.ending === null` in its own loop, so the bot bands were computed on the fixed behaviour and would not move. Tick counts and scores taken either side of that fix are therefore not comparable, which is the second reason it stays out of a dispatch whose first job is to take baselines. Found by the technical and game design gates on 2026-08-23.

**A recoverable fault is recorded and de-duplicated, because a persistent one fires every tick.** `checkStage` records what it saw only once both of its checks pass, so after a backwards phase index it compares against the stale value on every later tick and fires again, and an out-of-range freshness or level persists the same way. One row per tick would bury the run under a single repeated fault, so a fault record keeps the check's identity, the first tick it fired on, and the total count, which is what a persistent fault needs to stay diagnostically useful. **Recorded faults are the original run's history and a replay never rewrites them.** Invariant definitions and severity policy both change over time, so a replay reports the faults the tape carries and separately reports what today's checks say, and the two stay distinguishable rather than merging into one list. A replay must never abort a historical run because a check that was recoverable when it was recorded is fatal now, or a severity retune invalidates every tape ever written.

`Execution`'s lifetime is the run's, and it is created where the run is created. `checkStage`'s per-run history is a module-level `WeakMap<RunState, StageWatch>` today, and it becomes a field on `Execution` rather than a field on `RunState`. That supersedes the note on #45 that put it on `RunState`, with Mark's agreement that the newer architectural constraint outranks the earlier ruling, and the reason changed underneath that note: Hungry Grave ADR 0019 widens the witness fold, so what lives on `RunState` is now what a replay is checked against, and a phase watch is neither the run's identity nor something the rules mutate.

Two consequences of that move are load-bearing and neither is free. The `WeakMap` was giving lifetime away: the watch died with the run and every caller got correct stage history without knowing the mechanism existed, while `Execution` is held by `GameScreen`, which is pooled, and the first and third entries under **The screen pool** in `apps/hungry-grave/docs/lessons.md` are that a pooled screen leaks anything nobody explicitly clears and that a rendered check which only ever plays run one is structurally blind to it. So the test that plays a run, ends it and plays another is required rather than optional. And `checkInvariants` takes the watch as a **required** parameter, so an omission is a type error: made optional, the roughly forty-three direct `checkInvariants(state)` call sites would silently stop checking phase monotonicity and phase-tick reset, and the pair of calls in `invariants.test.ts` that exist precisely to exercise the watch would go green while checking nothing. The existing ordering rule survives the move unchanged: the watch is recorded after both checks and never before, or a rejected value is read as healthy on the next tick.

**This amends Hungry Grave ADR 0013.** That ADR puts the verification rig in `src/dev` and says it is never load-bearing in the shipped game. The harness now lives in `src/game`, because `executeTick` lives there and shipped `src/game` code may reach only `src/game`, and it runs in the shipped game on every tick. Everything else in 0013 stands as written: spec tests authored from the design record before they run against an implementation, the deterministic headless bot asserting the run's shape, the browser boot check, and feel evaluation staying the human's.

The cost of checking every tick was diagnosed, fixed and re-measured rather than accepted. The first measurement put `checkInvariants` at 298 to 372 microseconds a tick against a bare `step` at 16 to 30, and the diagnosis was allocation rather than arithmetic: `checkNoNaN` built one object literal plus an `Object.entries` array per entity per tick with every key a template literal, which is five string allocations per mob at 94 to 110 live entities. Rewritten to build a no-NaN message only when a number actually fails, the checks measure **23 to 37 microseconds a tick** on the same seeds and the same busiest ticks, roughly a tenfold drop and about what one bare `step` costs in the same pass at 17 to 25. A catch-up frame at `MAX_CATCHUP_TICKS` of fifteen therefore spends about **0.56 milliseconds** on checks rather than the **5.58** the first figures implied, and the harness entering the shipped bundle costs 4.3 kB raw and 1.3 kB gzipped. Both catch-up figures are fifteen ticks at the top of their band, fifteen times 37 microseconds against fifteen times 372, so the tenfold drop in the per-tick cost is the same tenfold in the frame cost and the two are comparable. Against a 16.7 millisecond frame that is **about 3.3%**, down from about a third of one. An earlier draft of this paragraph paired the top of the before-band with the middle of the after-band, which made the same drop read as 5.6 to 0.45 and produced a false "under 3% of a desktop frame"; the figures here are end-matched deliberately. Those are the numbers the always-on ruling stands on. The feedback path still holds and simply matters less at this size: slower ticks make more tick debt, more debt makes more catch-up ticks, and every one of them carries the check cost. **The open half is the phone.** Every figure here is a desktop reading, a phone is slower and is unmeasured, and the readings come off a deployed measurement build rather than off this document. Those figures are also hand-taken: the desktop numbers were read out of a temporary benchmark's output and copied in, and a phone reading is available today only off the `DEBT` label somebody watches and types back. Hungry Grave ADR 0018 rules that shape out as the standing workflow for performance evidence, which is a statement about what the instrument must produce rather than a reason to hold up a reading taken the only way currently available.

Ruled by Mark across the analytics and replay grill on 2026-08-22 and the three review gates on #48 on 2026-08-23. The argument is in [../design/analytics-and-replay-grill-2026-08-22.md](../design/analytics-and-replay-grill-2026-08-22.md).
